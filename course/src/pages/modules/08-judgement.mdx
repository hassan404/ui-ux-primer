## Why

Watch what your judgement does when it works: it notices something is off, says why, and holds a view of what better looks like. Each piece trains separately, and beginners skip the articulation step. A feeling you can name becomes a decision you can repeat; a feeling you cannot name stays luck.

Module 0 promised the taste gap closes. This is the mechanism. Every drill in this course made you commit, then articulate; the modules end, and the loop is yours to keep running on interfaces you meet. Whatever you feed the loop shapes the taste it grows. Train on shipped products that survived contact with users, on the tools you respect, on the boring excellence of a good settings page. A feed of portfolio shots optimizes for what wins likes in a feed, and screenshots never show the empty state, the error, the loading jank, which is to say the design. <span class="conf">practitioner consensus</span>

One prejudice to refuse early: taste has no required aesthetic. A dense flight-deck of a trading tool and a spare landing page can both be exactly right; each serves its user's glance. Taste is the fit between choices and purpose, and minimalism is one purpose among many. The developer who "adds whitespace until it looks designed" has learned a style, not judgement.

## The move

The critique card. Nine questions, in order, against any screen, yours or anyone's:

1. What is this screen's job, in one sentence?
2. Where did my eye land first, and is that the most important thing?
3. What does it want me to do next, and how fast did I know?
4. What happens when it is empty, loading, failing, or overflowing with real data?
5. Which gaps disagree with the scale, and did anyone choose that?
6. What would I remove?
7. Where did the maker decide, and where did they default?
8. Whose taste is this serving, the user's or the maker's?
9. Can I say why it works or fails, in sentences a colleague could act on?

Run it once right now, against the trial card from module 1, with a written answer to compare against at each step:

Then run it weekly on an interface you admire and one you resent. Written answers, a few sentences. The card feels mechanical for about a month, and then it stops being a card; the questions become reflexes, which was the plan.

And the copywork from the drill: pick an interface you respect, rebuild one screen with your own hands in your own stack, and write down three decisions you would have made differently before seeing theirs. This is how painters trained for centuries. It works on screens for the same reason. <span class="conf">old method, strong track record</span>

<span class="hatch">Every rule in this course is a 90% rule, and now you hold the tool that finds the other 10%. When your articulated reason beats the rule's reason, break the rule and write down why. The written why is what separates judgement from drift; drift is how the nineteen-pixel gaps got there.</span>

## Before you ship

- The critique card, weekly, in writing, on one screen you admire and one you resent.
- One copywork rebuild a month. Three sentences on what you found.
- Feed the loop shipped products, and study their unglamorous states.
- Breaking a rule requires a written why. Keep the whys; they are your taste, in versioned form.
