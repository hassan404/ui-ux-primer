## the repro

You built the screen. Every element on it earned its place — the title matters, the data matters, the button *really* matters. So you made them all clear. All legible. All... equal.

And now the screen reads like a log file with everything at ERROR level: technically informative, practically unreadable. When everything shouts, the reader hears nothing — they just feel tired and leave. Flat hierarchy is the most common bug in developer-built UIs, and the least visible from the inside, because *you* know where to look. You wrote it. Your users are seeing it cold, and their eyes land wherever the pixels tell them to.

## the root cause

Every screen has a job, and the test is brutal: **can you say the job in one sentence?** "Let the user pay an invoice." "Show whether the deploy is green." If you need a second sentence, you have a second screen hiding in the first — that's a different bug, and we'll meet it later.

Once you know the job, hierarchy has exactly one rule: **what the eye meets first must be what matters most.** Not what you built last. Not what was hardest. What matters — to the user, for the job.

The failure mode has a name: prominence–importance mismatch. The loudest element isn't the most important one. Watch how it feels from the outside:

## the fix

Hierarchy is not a talent. It's three levers, and you already have your hand on all of them:

1. **Size** — bigger reads first. The bluntest lever; two points is often plenty.
2. **Weight** — bold anchors, regular supports. Free structure, zero pixels of new space.
3. **Color** — near-black shouts, gray recedes. Contrast *is* volume.

Pull them one at a time and watch a flat card come back to life:

<LeverStepper client:visible />

The counterintuitive half of the skill: you'll usually make things matter **by making their neighbors matter less**. Can't make the total stand out? Don't enlarge it — gray out the labels around it. Amateur hierarchy adds volume; practiced hierarchy subtracts it. Labels especially: the word "Amount" is scaffolding, the number is the building. Data dark and firm, labels small and gray — almost never the reverse.

And buttons, the place this bites hardest: a screen gets **one** filled, high-contrast primary action. Secondary actions get outlines; the "are you sure you want to delete everything" tier gets a quiet text link. If two buttons are shouting, neither is primary — that's not emphasis, that's a race condition.

<span class="hatch">One primary <em>per decision point</em>, not per pixel. A long page can have several decision moments as you scroll. And "destructive gets red" can outrank "secondary gets an outline" — semantics may win over volume. The rule you're keeping: never two loudest things in one glance.</span>

## the tool

You don't need anyone's opinion to test hierarchy — you need worse eyesight, on demand. Squint (or step back, or blur a screenshot) until you can't read the words. Whatever still stands out *is* your hierarchy, whether you meant it or not. Designers call this the squint test; think of it as printing the stack trace of prominence and reading the top frame. <span class="conf">old designer folk-method, works</span>

Run it on the trial card: squinted, the loud detour button *owns* the screen and the real ask disappears completely. The blur can't read your intentions — only your pixels.

## the regression test

Prove the eye is installed — no annotations this time, just you:

Before you ship any screen, run these four:

- **One sentence:** what is this screen's job? (Can't say it? Split the screen, not the difference.)
- **Squint:** does what survives the blur match what matters?
- **One loudest thing** per glance — count your filled buttons.
- **Labels quieter than data.** Scaffolding never outranks the building.
