## why

What you just felt has a mechanism.

Your eye does not read a screen. It lands on it, wherever the contrast is strongest, and only then starts reading. The landing order is decided entirely by pixels: sizes, weights, colors. Importance has no vote unless someone gives it one.

So every screen carries two rankings: what matters most, and what is most prominent. When they match, the screen feels obvious. When they disagree, users feel lost and cannot say why. And when everything is equally loud, there is no landing point at all. The eye circles, gets tired, and leaves. That flatness is the most common bug in developer-built interfaces, and the hardest to see from the inside, because you already know where to look. Your users arrive cold.

Which ranking is right comes from the screen's job. Say it in one sentence: let the user pay an invoice. Show whether the deploy is green. If you need two sentences, you have two screens sharing one URL, and that is a different fix.

## the move

Hierarchy runs on three levers, and your hand is already on all of them:

1. **Size.** Bigger lands first. The bluntest lever; two points is often enough.
2. **Weight.** Bold anchors, regular supports. Structure at no cost in space.
3. **Color.** Near-black comes forward, gray steps back. Contrast is volume.

Pull them one at a time:

<LeverStepper client:visible />

The half nobody guesses: most of the time you make a thing stand out by quieting its neighbors. If the total will not read, resist enlarging it. Gray out the labels around it instead. Labels are scaffolding and values are the building: keep the word "Amount" small and gray, keep the number dark and firm, and almost never the reverse.

Buttons are where this bites hardest. A screen gets one filled, high-contrast action. Secondary actions get outlines. Rare or dangerous actions get quiet text links. Two filled buttons in one view means the screen has not decided, and the user inherits the indecision.

<span class="hatch">One primary per decision point, not per page: a long page can hold several decision moments as the user scrolls. And a destructive action may claim red even when it is secondary; semantics can outrank volume. What survives every exception: never two loudest things in one glance.</span>

## the tool

You can test hierarchy without anyone's opinion, including yours. Blur the screen and watch what survives. Squint, step back across the room, or use the toggle:

This is the trial card from the top of the module. Blurred, the comparison button owns it outright and the real ask vanishes. The blur reads pixels, not intentions, which is exactly what makes it trustworthy.

## before you ship

First, prove the eye works unassisted:

<ABFlicker demo="hierarchy" client:only="react" />

Then, on any screen, four checks:

- Say the screen's job in one sentence. If you cannot, split the screen.
- Squint. What survives should match what matters.
- Count the filled buttons per glance. One.
- Labels quieter than data, everywhere.
