## notice

Pull up the last screen you shipped. Look at it the way a stranger would: half a second, no context, no affection.

Something reads as off. You caught it before you could name it. Try to name it now and the words run out fast. The font is fine. The colors are fine. Every element survives inspection on its own, and the whole still looks homemade.

You have run this loop before. It usually ends with adding things: a border, a shadow, maybe an animation. The screen gets busier, the off feeling stays, and you close the laptop having quietly filed design under gifts you did not receive.

## why

Two facts change what that moment means.

First, the noticing is the skill. Seeing that a screen is wrong faster than you can explain it is the working core of design judgement, the thing people point at when they say someone "has an eye." Yours fired. What you are missing is the vocabulary that turns the feeling into a diagnosis, and the diagnosis into a fix. Vocabulary is learnable.

Second, the gap between what you can see and what you can make is normal. Ira Glass described it years ago: for a long stretch, your taste runs ahead of your ability, and the distance feels like proof you are not meant for the work. Most people quit inside that gap. The ones who get good recognized it as the entry point. <span class="conf">strong consensus</span>

One more belief has to go: that design starts with a blank page and inspiration. Watch a skilled designer begin a project and you will see nothing of the kind. Before a single screen exists, they choose a small set of values: a spacing scale, a type scale, one accent color and its shades. Then they apply those values everywhere, with a consistency that would look compulsive in any other context. If you have ever pulled magic numbers out into named constants, you already have the discipline this takes.

## the move

When a screen looks off and you cannot say why, check the gaps first. Spacing is the most common cause and the hardest to see directly, because no single gap is wrong. Each one was chosen reasonably, on its own, at some point. Together they do not relate, and your eye reads unrelated intervals as noise. <span class="conf">the community's most repeated diagnosis</span>

## how this course works

Each module makes you look at something first, then explains what your eye did, then hands you the moves, then leaves you a short checklist. You will be asked to commit to answers before anything is revealed. It is fine to guess. The reveal teaches either way.

Three things you can hold this course to:

- **It is finite.** Ten modules, about three hours, no signup, no streaks. It is built to be finished.
- **Rules come with their exceptions attached**, in the same breath, in boxes like the one above.
- **Claims are labeled by their evidence**: replicated research, strong convention, or folklore that works. They are not the same, and the labels will say so, like <span class="conf">this</span>.

## before you ship

- The off feeling is a signal. When it fires, trust it, then check spacing before anything else.
- Watch for the urge to add decoration. The fix is usually alignment or removal.
- You are not behind. The noticing already works. The rest of this course is the vocabulary.
