Every list, table, and dashboard in your product carries these states, plus a partial state (one item, or a thousand). The mock shows ideal. Real life opens on empty, waits on loading, and lands on error at the worst moment. Design the other states or ship whatever your framework does by default, which the user reads as your design anyway.

## why

A screen is a conversation, and conversations run on response. Three timing thresholds, measured and stable across decades of research: under 0.1 seconds feels instant; under 1 second keeps the flow, though the user notices the wait; past 10 seconds, attention leaves and needs a progress indicator with an estimate to return. <span class="conf">replicated research</span> Between those marks, perception beats stopwatch. An optimistic update (show the result now, reconcile behind the scenes) reads as instant even when the network is slow. A skeleton reads faster than a spinner in most tellings, though the study behind that claim is weaker than the blog posts citing it. <span class="conf">contested evidence</span>

The other law of conversation: people make mistakes, and your design sets the price of each one. Interfaces feel safe when actions are visible, reversible, and hard to trigger by accident. They feel dangerous when a slip costs real work. Safety is a feeling users develop about your product over time, and it decides how freely they explore it.

## the move

**Forms, where behavior bites hardest:**

- **Labels above fields, always present.** A placeholder that vanishes on focus deletes the question the moment you start answering. Placeholders are for format hints, never for the label itself.
- **Validate late, forgive early.** Flag a field when the user leaves it, never on the first keystroke; you would be marking their answer wrong while they write it. Once a field has flagged, clear the flag the moment the input turns valid.
- **Errors name the fix.** Compare:

<BeforeAfter demo="errorcopy" client:visible />

- **Cut fields.** Each one costs completions. If the backend can derive it, ask nothing.

**Destructive actions:** prefer undo to confirmation. A confirmation dialog interrupts everyone to protect against the rare slip, and habit defeats it within a week; the finger learns the "Yes" button's position. Undo protects the same slip after the fact and costs the careful majority nothing. Implement it by delaying the real side effect a few seconds while showing the action as done. <span class="conf">strong convention</span>

<span class="hatch">Confirmation stays right when undo is impossible: sending an email, charging a card, deleting an account with cascading data. Match the friction to the stakes; typing the project's name to delete it is honest friction for an irreversible act.</span>

**Feedback on everything touchable:** buttons show hover, press, focus, and disabled states; a submitted form shows progress on the button itself; a finished action confirms visibly. Fingers need 44px targets. Focus rings stay visible, because keyboards are how many users and every power user drives.

## before you ship

- Empty, loading, error, and partial states designed for every data view. Empty states teach; errors name the fix and keep the user's work.
- Labels above fields; validation on leave, forgiveness on fix.
- Undo over confirmation wherever undo is buildable.
- Every interactive element answers hover, press, focus, and disabled.
- Waits under 300ms show nothing; longer waits show progress where the user is looking.
