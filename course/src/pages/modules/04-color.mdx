## Why

Think of color as three dials, hue, saturation, and lightness, rather than a wall of swatches. Professional palettes turn the hue dial rarely and the lightness dial constantly. A typical polished product runs on one accent hue, a set of neutrals, and a few semantic colors, with five to nine lightness steps of each. The variety you admire in good interfaces is mostly shades, one hue at many lightnesses, doing hierarchy work. New hues are for new meanings. No study proves this one; every mature design system you can name obeys it.

The grey-on-color bug from the drill comes from ignoring the hue dial: grey carries no hue, so on a colored background it clashes and goes muddy. De-emphasizing text on color means keeping the background's hue and raising the lightness. The same logic runs through your neutrals. Pure grey next to a strong accent feels detached; greys tinted a few percent toward the accent hue feel like one family. This site's greys carry its teal ink, which is why the chrome feels whole.

One dial is measurable. Contrast between text and background computes to a ratio, and 4.5:1 is the floor for body text. That number is the WCAG standard, not an opinion. Your good monitor and good eyes are the wrong measuring tool; the ratio does not negotiate. Guess the floor by eye once:

## The move

Building a palette, in order:

1. **Pick one accent hue.** From the brand, or from taste; the discipline matters more than the hue.
2. **Generate five to nine lightness steps of it**, from near-white wash to near-black ink. These do buttons, links, focus rings, washes, and hovers.
3. **Tint your neutrals toward the accent hue**, a few percent of saturation. Text, borders, and backgrounds come from this ramp.
4. **Add semantic colors last**, one hue each for success, warning, and danger, each with a wash, a mid, and an ink step. Semantic red means danger; it stops meaning danger if it also means "brand accent" somewhere else.
5. **Check every text color against its background.** 4.5:1 body, 3:1 for large text.

In the hierarchy toolkit, color ranks after size and weight. A screen where color is the only ranking signal fails the squint test and fails colorblind users at the same time. Color marks; it should rarely be the sole carrier of meaning, and WCAG makes the same demand as a principle.

<span class="hatch">Working in OKLCH instead of HSL makes the lightness dial honest (HSL thinks yellow and blue at 50% are equally light; your eye disagrees), and modern CSS supports it. The palette logic is identical. And if your product ships a marketing brand with six hues, keep them on marketing surfaces; product chrome still runs on one accent plus neutrals.</span>

## Before you ship

- One accent hue, shades doing the work. New hues only for new meanings.
- Neutrals tinted toward the accent. No pure grey text on colored backgrounds; use a tint of the background hue.
- Every text color measured, 4.5:1 or better. The checker decides, your eye does not.
- Anything colored carries a second signal too: an icon, a weight, a label.
