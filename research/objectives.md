# Objectives — what the research phase must accomplish

*Written before reading any research. This is the yardstick; the raw inventory gets measured against it.*

---

## The one-sentence goal

Find everything needed to teach a developer **sound UX judgement** in the **shortest possible time** — then have the evidence to throw 80% of it away with confidence.

The inventory is not the course. It exists so that ruthless prioritization is *informed* rather than guessed.

---

## What the learner should walk away with

These are the outcomes the course must produce. Every candidate topic in the inventory will be judged by how directly it serves one of them.

1. **A working eye.** They can look at a screen — theirs or anyone's — and *see* what's wrong: misalignment, missing hierarchy, unclear next action, absent feedback. Noticing precedes fixing.
2. **A small set of load-bearing principles.** Not 200 terms — the ~20 ideas that explain most of what makes interfaces work: hierarchy, feedback, affordance, consistency, forgiveness, mental models, progressive disclosure, and their kin.
3. **Concrete default moves.** When they open an editor, they know what to do: a spacing scale, a type scale, one accent color, alignment first, states for empty/loading/error. Rules they can follow *before* they have taste, that produce taste through use.
4. **A judgement procedure.** A repeatable way to critique any screen ("what is this screen's job? what's competing with it?") so quality stops being a vibe and becomes a checklist they eventually internalize and discard.
5. **Calibrated humility.** Knowing what they can't know from their desk — when to test with a human, and how to do it in an afternoon, not a quarter.
6. **Vocabulary that unlocks the literature.** Enough terms, correctly understood, that NN/g articles, design system docs, and designers themselves become legible.

## What the inventory must answer

The research agents fan out so that, combined, they can answer:

- **Consensus:** Which topics appear in *nearly every* serious course, book, and syllabus? (That intersection is the 80/20 core — the strongest prioritization signal we can get.)
- **Structure:** How do the best teachers *sequence* this material? What do they put first, and why?
- **Pain:** What do developers *actually* struggle with, in their own words? (The course's empathy has to be earned from real frustrations, not imagined ones.)
- **Craft:** What are the concrete, followable rules — the ones that work before understanding does?
- **Behavior:** How should interfaces act, not just look — states, feedback, forgiveness?
- **Taste:** Is judgement teachable, and by what exercises? What do practitioners say actually built theirs?
- **Vocabulary:** What's the full term-space, and which fraction is load-bearing?
- **Validation:** What's the minimum honest way to check a design against reality?
- **Form:** How do the best learning experiences deliver material — and which format fits teaching *visual judgement*, a skill of seeing and comparing?

## Prioritization criteria (the "ruthless" part)

When synthesizing, a topic earns its place only by scoring on:

| Criterion | Question asked |
|---|---|
| **Frequency** | Does it appear across most independent sources? |
| **Leverage** | Does one idea explain/improve many situations? |
| **Actionability** | Can a developer apply it the same day? |
| **Irreversibility** | Does skipping it cause damage that's hard to notice or undo? |
| **Teachability** | Can it be conveyed in minutes, not weeks? |

A topic that scores high on frequency but low on actionability (e.g., design history) gets cut. A topic low on frequency but high on leverage and actionability (e.g., "squint test") can stay.

## Anti-goals

Just as binding as the goals:

- **Not** a designer-conversion program. The learner stays a developer; they get judgement, not a Figma career.
- **Not** comprehensive coverage of the field. Completeness of the *inventory*, ruthlessness of the *course*.
- **Not** tool training. No Figma tutorials, no framework-specific CSS.
- **Not** a lecture on process theater. Personas, journey maps, and double diamonds appear only if evidence shows a developer needs them.
- **Not** long. If the whole course can't be consumed in a few focused hours, we've failed the brief.

## Definition of done for this phase

- `raw/` holds the fan-out findings: principles, syllabi, community voices, visual craft, interaction patterns, taste-building, vocabulary, process, delivery formats — each cited.
- `synthesis.md` reconciles the raw findings against this document: the consensus core, the cut list, the sequence, and the gaps where sources disagree.
- `delivery.md` recommends the course's form, with the shortlist of formats judged by fit-to-purpose and build cost.
